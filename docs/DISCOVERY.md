# Discovering behaviour instead of copying it

Owner's rule (2026-11-15): **SMART and NETWORK are benchmarks, never teachers, opponents in training, or a source of data.**
Imagine SMART did not exist: the only opponent that is ours is RANDOM and ourselves. A player built by imitating SMART can
at best become SMART, which is still far from NETWORK, and it cannot invent a tactic that SMART does not have. So the
question this document answers is: *where does new behaviour come from?*

## The fixed point we measured

Four training runs (candidate CE, PPO, PPO + temperature, PPO + pairs) all land at 37-40% vs SMART, 93% vs RANDOM, 6-9%
vs NETWORK, and all play the same shape: 12 cards per game, phases 4/5 only, ~20% pass in phase 3, ~40% standard
projects (`docs/EXPERIMENTS.md`, "State of the player").

The reason is structural, not a loss-function detail:

- the policy is trained on games played by that same policy, so the data contains no line it would not play;
- the value function is trained on the outcomes of those same games, so it cannot know that a line it never plays is
  worth playing;
- the search only re-ranks BUILD_CARD / DRAFT_CARDS. The *strategic* decisions - which phase to call, whether to spend the
  phase-3 action on a standard project or on the engine, whether to stop - are pure policy, and that is where the shape
  of the game is decided.

Random *temperature* on the policy does not fix this either (measured: no change). Sampling candidates at temperature 0.3
explores near the same policy: 14 decisions per turn and the phase pick is one of them, so a game still looks like all
the others. Exploration has to be *at the level of the decision that defines the strategy*.

## The loop (no teacher anywhere)

1. **Explore the generator, not the player.** `-Dares.agent.explore=PICK_PHASE:1.0` makes the data-generating player
   draw that decision *uniformly at random* from its legal candidates, and only that one. Everything else is played by
   the current best player. The games that come out are legal, complete, and unlike anything the greedy player produces:
   phases 1/2/3 appear, engines get built, some of those games are won.
2. **Measure what paid.** `ml/explore_stats.py` reads the recorded mirror games (both seats are us, so the only difference
   between seats is the exploration) and relates the final margin to how each seat played: phases called, incomes, tags,
   colours, resources, milestones, awards. The data says which lines are better; nothing is assumed.
3. **Teach the critic the counterfactual.** `train_value.py` on those games (target: final margin, i.e. a dense signal that
   separates "won by 30" from "lost by 5") gives V the states and outcomes of engine play it has never seen.
4. **Let the improved policy be the target, not the guide.** With a critic that knows what engine states lead to, the
   policy step is a *policy gradient with that critic as the baseline* on data generated by uniform exploration of one
   decision head (`train_policy_ce.py --objective pg-uniform`): since the behaviour policy of those rows is exactly the
   uniform distribution, the importance ratio pi(a|s)/(1/K) = pi(a|s)·K is exact - this is REINFORCE, not an
   approximation of it.
5. **Close the loop.** Regenerate games with the improved player, keep the best by measurement, repeat. Benchmarks are
   only ever evaluated, never trained against.

## Why the exploration is defensible

It is not a strategy, it is the absence of one: a uniformly random legal legal candidate on a single decision head, for a
bounded share of the games. Nothing about cards, colours, phases or economics is encoded. It produces *counterfactual*
games - what would have happened had we played differently - which is exactly the information a player needs and the one
thing self-play of a single greedy policy can never produce.

## Acceptance (unchanged, owner's rule)

Every candidate first on `ml/check_player.py` (cards per game, which phases it picks, the phase-3 spend mix; degenerate =
<3 builds, >50% standard projects in phase 3, >80% of picks in one phase), then on strength. Effects below ~1.5 points
are measured on 10 000 games only - a 3 000-game evaluation (±1.6) cannot see them.
